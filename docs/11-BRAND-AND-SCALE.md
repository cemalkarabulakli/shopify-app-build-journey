# Brand & Scale — From "buy at 3, sell at 5" to a brand people come back to

> Sources: two Turkish YouTube talks, distilled into one ordered playbook.
> **Ali Aslan — [İzleyeceğiniz En Detaylı E-Ticaret Markalaşma Eğitimi](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cJ72F17W0FU)**
> (12 May 2026, 27 min). He says he has founded dozens of e-commerce brands in Türkiye and abroad and runs
> an agency that manages hundreds more (self-reported; see the profile in section 5). A teardown of four brands (Grüns, an unnamed men's testosterone
> brand he consulted for, AG1, IM8) around five pillars: **offer, ads, popup, landing page, email/SMS**.
> **Gürkanzone — [Premium Markalar Fiyat Savaşından Nasıl Kaçıyor?](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=UQ0RJR5wCLc)**
> (29 Aug 2026, 36 min). Why a high price is a *result* of value, not a starting decision, and the order
> of operations from a single test order to a premium brand.
> Every step is read twice — once for a store, once for the app this journey builds. An app is a product
> sold to merchants; the mechanics are the same. Watched and distilled: September 2026.

## 0. Four rules the steps rest on (Ali Aslan)

1. **"Buy at 3, sell at 5" is over.** Everyone knows how to dropship. In 2026 buyers compare more,
   marketplaces are one click away, and the only question that matters on your own site is:
   *why would they buy from you?*
2. **Three goals, in this order.** A sales curve that does not need a bigger ad budget every day.
   A second, third, tenth purchase from every customer (LTV, not one order). The right product told
   to the right audience in the right words.
3. **Sell a pain you have lived.** He wants a leather-goods brand because he carries leather every
   day; a rider should sell motorcycle gear. If neither you nor anyone around you has the problem,
   you will not sell it at scale. (Phase 5 of this map is the same rule for an app.)
4. **Your research shapes your brands.** The categories he studies globally are the ones that grow in
   his portfolio. Vision comes before creatives and before adding products — "every product sells
   once money is spent; the marketing angle is what makes it a brand or leaves it at dropshipping."

## 1. Mindset: premium is a result, not a price (Gürkanzone)

The whole talk is one shift: **a premium brand does not persuade the customer to pay more. It changes
how the customer compares you to the cheap alternative.** Once your product sits in the same mental
bucket as a rival's, the cheaper one wins and you spend your life defending the price with discounts.

- **The airport bag.** Store A: a 900 ₺ bag, photos, dimensions, pocket count, a permanent "-40%" tag.
  Store B: the same bag at 4,500 ₺, but the page shows a frequent flyer's problems — laptop protected,
  shirts uncreased, fits the cabin bin, and what the brand does when something breaks. A sells a bag;
  B sells a calmer, more controlled trip for one specific person. Same object, different meaning, so
  the prices are not compared either.
- **Dark photos and gold accents do not make you premium.** They make you *look like* premium sites.
  The unanswered question is still: *why should the customer pay you more?* If there is no clear answer,
  expensive packaging and a warehouse full of stock do not make a premium brand.
- **"Higher quality" is not a difference.** Everyone says it; nobody sells "average quality". Quality
  only counts when it is *visible and measurable*: lasts longer (prove it), saves time (show which task
  and how much), reduces risk (warranty, reliable delivery, real after-sales). Premium is the set of
  outcomes the customer can understand and measure, not adjectives.
- **What people actually pay for:** less uncertainty, less effort, a lower chance of choosing wrong.
  "Will it look like the photo? Will it break in two months? Will anyone answer if it does?" A strong
  brand removes questions. That is why a 20% cost difference can carry a 3–4× price difference.
- **Ads cannot hide a missing product forever.** Good ads sell a bad product faster at first; then
  returns, complaints and a damaged reputation grow at the same speed. Marketing stops being growth
  and becomes a machine for distributing disappointment.

### The three layers, in order

1. Create a real value the customer cares about.
2. Make that value visible.
3. Attach the product to a bigger meaning in the customer's life — "this was made for people like me."
   A running shoe also says "I am disciplined"; a coffee machine turns a morning into a ritual. Premium
   brands do not hand out a new identity; they make visible the person the customer already wants to be.

### Three hard questions about your customer

Demographics ("women 25–40, big city, upper-middle income") say who someone is, not why they buy. Two
women with identical profiles buy the same product for opposite reasons; one loves a 70% discount, the
other starts doubting the real price. Ask instead:

1. How does my customer see themselves today?
2. Who do they want to become?
3. **Who do they not want to look like?** People buy to leave a group as much as to join one — the new
   business owner who does not want amateur-looking gear, the beginner runner escaping the "starts and
   quits" self, the parent who wants to be sure they chose right, not to show off.

A planner sold as "120 gsm paper, hard cover, 200 pages, 4 colours" is a notebook. The same planner
designed for people who plan for three days and quit — simplified layout, short methods, copy about a
messy life getting slightly better — is a system for keeping promises to yourself. The ads then show
the Monday the plan collapsed, not the product; the customer sees themselves before they see the item.

### Things that break the story

- **"Family" is not community.** Calling followers family builds nothing. Community is customers bonding
  with *each other* around a shared behaviour: a running brand's group runs and levelled programmes, a
  coffee brand teaching brewing until customers become connoisseurs. Then they invent their own words,
  repeat rituals and buy the next product to stay part of the world (Apple).
- **A club for everyone means nothing to anyone.** Say plainly who the brand is the right choice for.
  Some people will say "not for me" — that is the goal. The danger is everyone filing you under
  "not bad" and nobody saying "exactly for me".
- **The founder story only counts if the founder's problem equals the customer's problem.** Nobody cares
  about your childhood dream. If you have no story, the strongest story is how customers used the
  product: where they were before, what they struggled with, what changed after.
- **Brand debt.** A 2,000 ₺ product sold at 999 ₺ for 300 days a year costs 999 ₺; the crossed-out
  figure is decoration. Rolling 30% → 40% → 50% campaigns teach one lesson: never pay full price here.
  Cash comes in today, priced out of tomorrow's trust. Then the campaign ends and sales stop, because the
  product is now the discount. Discounts are fine with a *reason that starts and ends* (season end,
  bundles, loyal customers, a birthday). Fake countdowns and "last 2 left" with hundreds in stock are
  not sales psychology; they are gambling with trust.
- **The weakest touchpoint sets the brand's value.** Calm, trustworthy ads; then a site with a
  5-minute countdown, a package thrown in a bag, no message when shipping slips, a support reply three
  days later. Every touchpoint whispers something. The package says "we thought about this detail too";
  tracking updates say "we are watching until it reaches you"; easy returns say "we trust our product".
  Premium does not mean spending more everywhere. It means removing uncertainty and carelessness at the
  points the customer cares about.
- **Not every product should go premium.** In some categories the customer's priority is lowest price,
  fastest delivery, easiest access. Cheap-and-high-volume is an excellent model if the unit economics
  hold. Premium is not the higher league; it is a different game with different rules — cost, speed and
  volume win the value game; difference, trust, experience and consistency win the premium game. Ruin
  starts when you play both: "we are not for everyone" on Monday, "-70% for everyone" on Tuesday.

### The order he would follow from zero

Right steps in the wrong order lose money: a custom box before the product is validated, a brand
identity before knowing the customer, stock before the sales system works.

1. **Never fall in love with the product.** Treat it as a hypothesis. Sell it in single orders, try
   different ad messages, and look for a *repeating pattern*, not revenue: which problem do they buy it
   for, which ad converts, what do they ask before buying, what do they love on unboxing, which unmet
   expectation causes returns.
2. **Do not chase everyone.** Pick the strongest use-case from the tests. Students liked the travel bag,
   but 2–3-day business travellers bought most — position for them: no checked luggage, laptop safe,
   organised on short trips. A brand gets stronger by meaning more to one person, not by widening.
3. **Improve the product in the direction customers tell you.** Marketing's job is to carry "why they
   buy" and "where it hurts" back to product: reinforce the laptop sleeve, swap the zipper, show which
   cabin sizes it fits. Now it is no longer a supplier item with a logo; it is a solution shaped by one
   customer's feedback. Real premium starts here — not in expensive details nobody notices.
4. **Turn every claim into proof.** "Durable" → show the test. "Waterproof" → show it in use. "Cabin
   size" → publish the dimensions. Every frequent question becomes content; every objection becomes a
   section on the product page; real customer experiences replace adjectives. A premium brand does not
   ask for blind belief; it makes believing easy.
5. **Build the identity from shared customer behaviour**, not from cool words at a desk: what they
   value, which problem they hate, how they see themselves, what change they describe. The bag brand
   sells being prepared and in control; the ads show the person at the carousel, in the hotel room, in
   the meeting without creased clothes — the product's *result*, never the product alone.
6. **Carry the promise into operations.** Site, ads and support speak with one voice. Delivery time is
   stated, return terms are not hidden, a problem finds someone who takes responsibility, the package is
   not the most expensive box but never a random bag either.
7. **Grow in controlled batches.** After single orders prove the product: 20–50 units, faster delivery,
   better QC, lower cost. Do not fill a warehouse after one good ad week; wait until CAC, return rate,
   profit per unit and sales velocity are consistent for a while. His April brand went 10 → 20 → 50 →
   200 → 1,000–2,000 units, reordering weekly. Premium price and premium stock are *earned* inside the
   model, step by step.

**Brand-health questions** (beyond ROAS and CAC, which still matter): do customers buy at full price;
is the return rate rising and why; do they reorder; is branded search growing; do they recommend you;
what share of sales depends on running a campaign? An ad that converts today but attracts discount
hunters or sets a false expectation kills the second order. Judge ads by the memory they leave, not only
by the day's revenue.

**The logo-off test.** Open your site, hide the logo, the brand name and the About page. Show a few
people only the product, photos, message, proof, pricing and experience, and ask: *why could this be
more expensive than its rivals?* Similar answers with no prompting → a clear difference exists. "Design
/ quality / packaging / probably the brand" → no shared value yet. Nobody finds a reason → your first
problem is not a logo, a box or a bigger ad budget; it is designing why the customer should pay you more.

- **App:** every line above holds for a Shopify app. The comparison frame is "another €19 app" unless
  the listing shows the merchant's Tuesday problem. Brand debt is the permanent "50% off first 3
  months". The weakest touchpoint is the support reply time. The logo-off test is the App Store listing
  with the app name hidden.

## 2. The steps (Ali Aslan)

### Step 1 — Pick the audience, then the pain you can name

Not "men / women / athletes" — that targeting stopped working, for him too. Ask: *who has the problem
this product ends, and what does that problem feel like on a Tuesday?*

- **Store:** list every life situation your product enters. A greens gummy enters "parent worried
  about a picky eater", "someone losing weight", "gut trouble", "athlete recovering".
- **App:** list the merchant situations, not the merchant sizes. "Subscription churn after the
  third box", "Plus store with a migration next quarter", not "SMB vs Plus".

### Step 2 — One product, one funnel per audience

Grüns runs six funnels for the same jar. The home page introduces the brand and its variants with
stats, and does **not** push a purchase. Each funnel page speaks to one audience's pain: the kids
page is about parental worry, not about the product; the weight-loss page and the gut-health page
sell the identical gummy to different fears; a fourth version targets athletes.

- **Rule:** the product page explains the product; the funnel page explains the *person's problem*.
  Turkish e-commerce still puts everything on the product page. Don't.
- **Store:** ship the general home page first, then one landing page per audience from Step 1.
  Ads point at the funnel page, never at the product page.
- **App:** one App Store listing, plus one landing page per merchant pain. Cold outreach links to the
  pain page. (The `/switch` page on this site is exactly this pattern.)

### Step 3 — Inside one audience, test which pain converts

The testosterone brand was **one** audience (men) with three candidate pains: sex life, muscle, or
weight. Three pages, same product, paid traffic split, watch conversion. Step 2 tests *audiences*;
this step tests *pain points* inside an audience. When the page hits the real pain, price, shipping
time and "how is it made" stop mattering.

- **Done when:** you can say which single pain outconverted the rest, with the number.

### Step 4 — The offer, and the discount that gets noticed

Discounts no longer carry meaning: 10%, 15%, 50%, 70% all read the same. He still discounts, but with
odd numbers — **52%, 23%, 81%** — because an unfamiliar number registers subconsciously. Rotate them
across Meta ads, Google ads and the popup.

- **Trap he fell into:** changing the discount changes the margin. Keep price, discount and
  profitability in one sheet and update all three together.
- **App:** the same applies to trial length and founding-member pricing. "23% off for the first 30
  merchants" beats "20% off". Then check the margin against your support time.

### Step 5 — Proof on the product page: videos with a label

Grüns' product page has a row of short videos, each with a one-phrase caption above it — *value*,
*benefits*, *ingredients* — so the visitor knows what they will get before pressing play. Don't post
generic UGC; every video has to carry a fact. His own conversion rate rose visibly once he added
labelled UGC to product pages.

- **Food / supplement rule:** say how it is made, what is inside and who uses it — on the page, in
  the emails, everywhere. Buyers of anything they eat want certainty about ingredients.
- **App:** the "videos" are 20-second screen recordings labelled *setup*, *what merchants see*,
  *the number it moved*. Merchant quotes with store names are your ingredients list.

### Step 6 — Own the data: email is a channel, not a newsletter

The metric his agency watches first: **how many people are in the list?** Brands chase Meta and
Google budgets and forget the channel with zero ad spend, addressed to people who already know them.
His Turkish brands make email compete with paid ads, on Klaviyo.

- **Cadence:** not weekly, not "3–4 times a month". Daily or every other day. The brand he showed
  sent about 6 emails on 8 April, 4 on 9 April, 3–4 on 10 April. Some will unsubscribe; don't cross
  into harassment; but stay in front of the buyer.
- **Welcome flow:** Grüns sends five emails to a new contact: the discount, how it differs from
  competitors, what is inside, the community around it, then the ask.
- **Daily campaigns** to buyers also introduce other products; quality of data decides the angle
  (an adult who bought for themselves can be shown the kids' version; a parent gets content on why
  a child benefits from a daily routine).
- **Automations he builds for every brand:** added to cart but no purchase; reached checkout, typed
  payment details, left; bought two of five items in the cart. Each is a rule, each is a flow.
- **App:** a merchant who installed and did not finish setup, one who hit the paywall, one whose
  trial ends in three days. Same three flows. The list is every install email, ever.

### Step 7 — SMS: short, factual, occasionally funny

Four SMS he showed, each one fact: what is inside; how it compares to a named rival; the benefits;
how it differs from the rest of the category. His own campaign used humour to stick — a "Day 6"
message with a muscular emoji next to a stick-thin one.

- **App:** SMS is rarely right for merchants; the equivalent is the one-line in-app banner or a
  Slack/community ping. Same rule: one fact per message.

### Step 8 — Put a face on it

**IM8** (David Beckham's supplement brand) is founder marketing done in the open: the founder is the
creative. **AG1** wins on ad volume and creative variety. Be the face of your own brand — "the founder
of this brand is me" — and use yourself in the ads.

- **App:** this site is the founder marketing. The journal *is* the ad creative.

### Step 9 — Spy on the brands you admire, on a schedule

- Keep a **separate email address** subscribed to every competitor and every brand whose marketing
  you envy. The flows in this talk all came from that inbox.
- Open their **Meta Ad Library** and count: how many ads are active, how long each has run, which
  months they scale, and whether the creatives are static, dynamic or UGC.
- **App:** subscribe to the top ten apps in your category with a dev store, install them, read
  their onboarding emails and their App Store review replies.

### Step 10 — Learn by running, buy help when stuck

Try your first store yourself; YouTube already holds most of the information. Pay for training or
mentorship only once a brand is live and earning and you hit a wall — then the investment pays for
itself. Before that, it is buying information you could have found.

## 3. Applied to this business: a software product sold to merchants

This journey sells software, not gummies. Three things are on sale today: the **Zero-Churn Switch**
app (flat $299/mo subscription billing for Shopify, migration done for you, five founding slots at a
price locked for life), the **VIP** membership around the build, and the **journal** itself, which is
the ad creative. Every rule above maps onto them without changing a word of the logic.

### The comparison frame (Gürkanzone → the listing)

- **Default frame:** "another subscription app, $299 vs Recharge's $99 starter". In that bucket the
  cheaper one wins and every sales call is a price defence.
- **Our frame:** "the percentage you stop paying." Recharge Pro is $499 + 1% + $0.19 per transaction; the
  page does the math on the merchant's own invoice. The product is not an app, it is *the end of a tax
  that grows with your success*. That is the 4,500 ₺ bag: same category, different meaning, so the price
  is not compared against a $99 plan.
- **Logo-off test for SaaS:** hide the app name on the listing and the `/switch` page. Ask three
  merchants why this could cost more than Seal or Skio. Expected shared answer: "flat price locked for
  life plus someone else does the migration". If they say "nicer UI", the difference is not visible yet.

### Audiences and pains → one landing page per pain (Ali Aslan steps 1–3)

The outreach CSV already segments the 1–2★ reviewers of Recharge, Seal, Skio and Subscription
Payments. The pains in those reviews are the funnels:

| Pain heard in reviews | Funnel page and first line | Proof that page needs |
|---|---|---|
| **Fees grow with revenue** ("over 10% of our recurring revenue") | "Every dollar Recharge skims, you keep" — the invoice calculator | Their own numbers, no estimates |
| **Locked in, can't move subscribers** | "Leave in 14 days, every subscriber reconciled" | Dry-run import report, refund-from-our-pocket guarantee |
| **Support is a bot, days to reply** | "A human answers, and it's the person who built it" | Reply-time log published in the journal |
| **Billing bugs** (double charges, wrong shipping) | "Built on Shopify's native contracts, your data stays in your store" | Reconciliation checklist, changelog |

Step 3 is the outreach itself: same product, four DM angles, count replies and deposits per angle.
The kill line in the playbook (3+ deposits go, 0–1 after 250 sends kill) is the pain-point test.

### The offer and brand debt (step 4 + Gürkanzone)

- **What is already right:** "$299 flat, locked for life, public price $499 after five" is a discount
  with a reason that starts and ends. It is the opposite of brand debt. Never turn it into a rolling
  "50% off this month". If a merchant hesitates, add proof or scope (a longer guarantee, migration of a
  second store), never a lower number.
- **Odd numbers:** "5 slots" reads better than "10". A $500 deposit reads better than $499; keep
  the deposit round and the monthly price odd.
- **VIP:** one trial (7 days), one reason. Founding-member price "locked forever" is the same mechanic;
  don't dilute it with coupon codes.

### Proof (step 5 + Gürkanzone step 4)

- Videos on the listing and on `/switch`, each labelled with one phrase: *the migration dry run*,
  *what your subscriber sees*, *the invoice before and after*, *a support reply in 40 minutes*.
- Every objection from the reviews becomes a section on the page: "what if a subscriber is double
  billed?" (refund plus 12 months free), "can I leave?" (native contracts, yes, in a day).
- Journal entries with real numbers are the ingredients list. Merchants buying billing software want
  to know what is inside exactly as supplement buyers do.

### Own the list (step 6)

- **The list is** every prospect emailed, every install email, every VIP email, every journal
  subscriber. Count it weekly; it is the first metric on the adventure card, not installs.
- **Welcome flow, five emails** to a new install or VIP: what you stopped paying, how the migration
  works, what merchants who switched say, the community around the build, the ask (book the call or
  finish setup).
- **Three automations:** installed but did not connect a store; reached the deposit page and left;
  trial ending in 3 days. Then daily or every-other-day journal emails to the whole list; the journal
  is the campaign calendar, one entry, one email.
- **Merchant SMS is wrong; the in-app banner and the private channel are the SMS.** One fact each.

### Identity, community, founder (Gürkanzone + step 8)

- **Who it is for:** subscription brands past ~300 subscribers who refuse to pay a percentage of
  their revenue to a billing tool and want a person, not a bot, on the other end. **Who it is not for:**
  a store with 20 subscribers that needs free, and an enterprise that wants an SLA and a CSM. Say
  both on the page; the "not for me" from the wrong merchant is the goal.
- **The three questions:** merchants see themselves as operators drowning in tools; they want to be
  the founder who runs a lean, profitable stack; they do not want to look like the store that got
  locked in and taxed. The copy speaks to the third.
- **Community is merchants helping merchants**, not a Discord called "family": founding merchants'
  catalogs set the roadmap (already promised), a shared migration checklist, a monthly call where they
  compare retention numbers.
- **Founder marketing is the site.** The journal, the numbers, the Fog Dragon. IM8 puts Beckham in
  the ad; this puts the builder's log in the ad. Do not hire a faceless brand voice.

### Operations set the price (Gürkanzone step 6)

The weakest touchpoint for SaaS is support reply time and migration day. "14 days, white glove,
refund from our pocket" is the promise; the operation has to repeat it under pressure: a reply-time
target published on the page and measured in the journal, a migration runbook, a status page.
A single three-day silence undoes the flat-price story.

### Controlled batches (Gürkanzone step 7 + Ali Aslan step 10)

- Five founding merchants by hand (the "single orders"). Then 20 at the public price with the runbook.
  Only then automate the migration and open the App Store listing wide.
- Do not build the pause/swap/B2B roadmap before a deposit asks for it. Stock in SaaS is features
  nobody paid for.
- Wait for CAC, churn, support hours per merchant and trial-to-paid to be steady for a while before
  raising spend.

### Brand-health metrics for this model

| Question | SaaS metric | Where it is logged |
|---|---|---|
| Do they buy at full price? | Share of paid merchants on the public $499, not a founding deal | Billing mirror |
| Is the return rate rising? | Churn within 60 days of migration, and why | Journal, monthly |
| Do they reorder? | Renewal after month 12, VIP yearly renewals | Billing mirror |
| Is branded search growing? | "zero churn switch" queries, direct visits to `/switch` | Analytics |
| Do they recommend you? | Installs and deposits that came from another merchant | Outreach CSV `reply` source |
| How much depends on campaigns? | Deposits that needed a discount to close | Outreach CSV |

### Spying, on a schedule (step 9)

Install the top ten subscription apps on a dev store with a separate inbox. Read their onboarding
emails, their trial-end emails and their replies to 1★ reviews. The review pages already scraped in
`outreach/` are the Meta Ad Library of this category: count active listings, feature cadence, and
which pains keep repeating month after month.

## 4. Use cases: brands founded 2020 or later, read for the mechanics

Only brands founded in 2020 or later with public numbers. Revenue marked *est.* is a journalist's or a
database's estimate, not a company disclosure. Researched 4 September 2026; sources at the end.

### Grüns — greens gummies, US, founded 2023
| When | Milestone |
|---|---|
| Aug 2023 | First sale; $1.8M pre-seed |
| ~Oct 2024 | Profitable in month 14, DTC only |
| Dec 2024 – Apr 2025 | Sprouts, Target (1,600 stores), Walmart (1,900); ~60 staff |
| May 2025 | $35M Series B at a $500M valuation, "nine-figure ARR" |
| Aug 2025 | ~$300M annualised revenue |
| Apr 2026 | Unilever acquires for ~$1.2B, closed 1 June 2026 |

- **Moves:** one product, 99+ landing pages matched to the ad angle (gut health, GLP-1 support, fibre,
  "vs green powder", perimenopause); 776 active Meta ads in one 30-day window; six customer avatars,
  each with its own page, testimonials and creative. Post-purchase stack: "3 pouches for the price of
  2" on a 12-week subscription, then a second one-click upsell, AOV $40.80 → $86.50. The founder
  grows "as fast as the world lets me at my CAC ceiling."
- **Copy:** this is Ali Aslan's Step 2 at scale. One hero SKU, a page per pain, the commit-upsell on the
  thank-you page. For the app: one listing, a page per merchant pain, the annual plan on the welcome page.

- **Frameworks that map onto this scroll:**
  - **Value Equation** — Value = (Dream outcome × Perceived likelihood) ÷ (Time delay × Effort). Software
    wins on the denominator; price on the numerator. Gürkanzone's "less uncertainty, less effort" is
    the same equation in Turkish.
  - **Grand Slam Offer** — starving crowd, value stack, risk-reversing guarantee, honest scarcity,
    premium price; make it incomparable rather than cheaper. The Zero-Churn Switch page is built on it.
  - **Core Four** — warm outreach, content, cold outreach, paid ads; then lead-getters (referrals,
    affiliates, agencies). Ali Aslan's five pillars are the paid-ads and content quadrants filled in.
  - **More → Better → New** — do more of what works, then improve it, only then add something new.
  - **Volume is the problem** — the Rule of 100: 100 outreach messages, or 100 minutes of content, or
    $100 of ads, every day. The outreach playbook's 25 sends a day is a quarter of it.
  - **Charge more** — premium price funds a better product and attracts better customers; cutting price
    is the vicious cycle. Gürkanzone's "brand debt" is the same warning.
  - **Give away the secrets, sell the implementation** — this site exactly: the journal is free, the
    migration is paid.
  - **Money Models** — attraction, upsell, downsell (change terms, not price), continuity; acquisition
    should pay for itself within 30 days. The $500 deposit is the attraction offer; the flat plan is

## 6. Read as a builder: where the money is

| Step | Store metric it moves | App metric it moves | Phase on the map |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1–3 Audience & pain funnels | Conversion rate per landing page | Install rate from outreach | 5, 8 |
| 4 Offer | Margin × conversion | Trial → paid | 7 |
| 5 Labelled proof | Product-page conversion | Listing → install | 8 |
| 6 Email flows | Repeat purchase, LTV | Activation, trial conversion, churn | 6, 7 |
| 7 SMS / in-app | Recovery of abandoned carts | Setup completion | 6 |
| 8 Founder face | CAC | CAC | this site |
| 9 Spying | Ad efficiency | Positioning | 5 |

Both talks land on one sentence: **the second sale is the brand.** Every step above either lowers the
cost of the first sale or raises the odds of the second.

## 7. Done when

- The logo-off test passes: three people give the same reason your product could cost more.

- Two funnel pages live for the same product, each aimed at a different pain, with a conversion
  number for each.
- A five-email welcome flow sending, plus the three abandonment automations.
- One repeat purchase (or one trial-to-paid conversion) you can trace to an email, not to an ad.
- A separate inbox with at least ten competitor newsletters in it, and one Ad Library note per rival.
