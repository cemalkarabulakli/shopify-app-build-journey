# The Apps Merchants Actually Use — and How Each One Started

> [12-Problem Selection](12-PROBLEM-SELECTION.md) is the method for finding a painkiller problem.
> This file is the evidence that the method works: for every app merchants actually have installed,
> the story of **what its founder saw, what v1 did, and how the first hundred customers arrived**.
>
> Read it for one thing only — the shape of the starting point. Almost none of these began as a
> platform, a vision or a category. Each began as one person watching a specific problem cost
> someone specific money.
> Researched: September 2026. Every number carries a source; estimates are marked.

---

## 0. How to read this file

Three rules, or the stories will mislead you:

1. **Survivorship.** These are the winners. For every Klaviyo there are a thousand apps with the same
   origin story and no ending. The story tells you what a good *start* looks like, never what a good
   outcome is guaranteed by.
2. **The era matters.** Bold and Recharge started when the App Store was nearly empty; a 2026 founder
   cannot repeat a 2013 land-grab. What still transfers is the **shape**: a named pain, a narrow v1,
   a hand-built first hundred.
3. **Read the middle, not the exit.** The valuation is the least useful sentence in each story. The
   useful sentence is always the one about what they did before anyone was paying.

## 2. The stories

### Klaviyo — email that could finally see the store

- **Founded** 2012, Boston, by Andrew Bialecki and Ed Hallen.
- **The origin:** Bialecki was helping a friend who ran a Shopify store. The email tools of the day
  could not use the store's real browse and purchase data in real time — they sent lists, not
  behaviour. The wedge was the database, not the email editor.
- **The merchant's sentence:** "My email tool doesn't know what my customers actually did on my site."
- **Where it got to:** FY2025 revenue **$1.2B**, up 32%, 193K customers (company results, 10 Feb 2026).
- **Copy this:** the boring layer underneath was the product. Everyone else was competing on templates.

### Judge.me — the same thing, priced for people who could not pay $199

- **Founded** 2015, bootstrapped.
- **The origin:** review apps were, in the founder's framing, generally overpriced at around $199/month,
  even though reviews clearly moved sales. Judge.me launched at **$15/month**.
- **The merchant's sentence:** "I know I need reviews. I can't justify $199 a month for them."
- **Where it got to:** $4M revenue in 2021 (founder interview); $11.2M in 2024 (*estimate*). Today the
  single most-installed app on Shopify at **20.5%** of all stores.
- **Copy this:** the pain was not "no review app exists". It was the price of the existing one. Being
  the affordable version of a proven category is a real strategy — and it produced the #1 install base.

### Gorgias — a pivot that the users chose

- **Founded** 2015.
- **The origin:** the founders built a templated-reply Chrome extension for anyone who typed the same
  message repeatedly. About **70% of the people using it turned out to be ecommerce support agents**,
  so they rebuilt it as a helpdesk that put Shopify order data inside the ticket.
- **The merchant's sentence:** "My support agent has to leave the ticket and dig in the admin to answer
  'where is my order'."
- **Where it got to:** ARR $25M (2022) → $51M (2023) → $69M (2024); $530M valuation, May 2024.
- **Copy this:** they read who was actually using the generic tool and followed them into a niche. The
  pivot was a discovery, not a brainstorm.

### Loop Returns — one operational pain at two named brands

- **Founded** 2017.
- **The origin:** Chubbies and Allbirds were spending, in the founders' description, far too much time
  and money processing returns by hand.
- **The merchant's sentence:** "Returns are eating my team's week and I still lose the revenue."
- **Where it got to:** $22M revenue 2022; $53.3M in 2024 (*estimate*); 5,000+ brands. Notably, returns
  is the one post-purchase category still **growing** (+16.5% YoY) while tracking apps shrink.
- **Copy this:** it started with two specific stores and one specific weekly cost, not a market study.

### Skio — the incumbent's architecture was the opening

- **Founded** 2020 by Kennan Davison.
- **The origin:** existing subscription apps relied on what he called hacky workarounds on Shopify.
  The wedge was structural: the incumbent's model and architecture could not be changed easily.
- **The merchant's sentence:** "My subscription app fights the platform, and I pay a percentage for it."
- **Where it got to:** $10M ARR and profitable within 3 years; sold to **Recharge for $105M cash** at
  $32M ARR, having raised only $8M (Apr 2026).
- **Copy this:** this is the closest analogue to this repo's own offer, and it is the clearest proof
  that a structural weakness in the incumbent beats a feature advantage. The buyer was the incumbent.

### Privy — two years of Friday mornings

- **Founded** Boston, by Benjamin Jabbawy, solo. Venture-backed: $1.7M seed (2013), $4.25M Series A
  (2018). *Sources disagree on the founding year — Crunchbase says 2011, a 2021 founder interview says
  2014.*
- **The origin:** the technical child of small-business owners, the one both parents came to when they
  needed a website or an ad account. v1 was a self-serve **promotions** tool, not a popup tool. He
  pivoted by watching what people actually valued: they loved it because it built an email list faster
  than anything else they had, not for the promotions.
- **The merchant's sentence:** "Traffic comes to my store, leaves, and I have no way to reach them again."
- **How the first hundred arrived — the most copyable part of this whole file.** Before the App Store
  listing, he walked customers through pasting a code snippet by hand. Then, **every Friday morning for
  at least two years**: Facebook groups (Shopify Entrepreneurs, 100K+ members), the Shopify, Squarespace
  and Mailchimp forums, and weekly Quora searches for the same terms, answering in detail. Plus any
  podcast that would have him — his reasoning was that even 100 listeners is 100 people hearing the
  story. Reported trajectory: ~50 installs in month one, then roughly doubling monthly (100 → 200 → 400
  → 800), eventually 12,000+ installs a month from Shopify alone.
- **Where it got to:** acquired by **Attentive**, 4 June 2021, terms undisclosed. A "$120M exit" figure
  circulates on a founder profile but neither company confirmed it (*unverified*).
- **Copy this:** distribution is a weekly habit, not a launch event. Two years of Friday mornings spent
  where merchants were already asking the question his app answered. Note what this is *not*: it is not
  a growth hack, a viral loop or an ad budget. It is one person, one recurring calendar slot.

### Oberlo — automate the chore you do every morning

- **Founded** September 2015, Vilnius, by five co-founders including Tomas Slimas.
- **The origin:** Tomas had failed at ecommerce for about three years first — in his words it was
  painful and did not go well, to the point he decided it was not for him. Then one store worked and
  generated $3 million in revenue in a single year. The app came out of running it: every day they were
  exporting orders from Shopify and sending suppliers a CSV. v1 was crude — products copied off
  AliExpress and added to the store by hand. It was originally called **Ali Importer**.
- **The merchant's sentence:** "Every morning I re-key products and CSV my orders to suppliers by hand."
- **Where it got to:** acquired by **Shopify in 2017** for a commonly reported ~$15M (*never confirmed
  by Shopify — treat as estimate*). Still had roughly 100,000 active stores when Shopify announced it
  was shutting it down; removed from the App Store 12 May 2022, killed 30 June 2022.
- **Copy this, and take the warning with it:** automate the chore you personally do every morning. Then
  remember that platform dependency cuts both ways — the acquirer that made Oberlo also switched it off
  while 100,000 stores were still using it.

### Printful — sell the new business to the list the old one built

- **Founded** 2013, Latvia (with a Los Angeles garage as the first production floor), by Lauris Liberts
  and Davis Siksnans inside Draugiem Group. Bootstrapped for **eight years**; first outside money was
  $130M in 2021.
- **The origin:** Liberts ran Startup Vitamins, selling posters and tees to founders, and was printing,
  packing and shipping them himself. The outsourcing options were worse — Siksnans says the suppliers of
  2012 missed on speed and quality, and crucially had no API, so there was no way to automate the order
  process. They bought a printer, put it in the garage, and learned to print. Even industrially it was
  slow: their first Kornit press in 2015 took six months to learn to use properly.
- **The merchant's sentence:** "I want branded merch without buying inventory, and every supplier is
  slow, ugly, or has no API."
- **How the first hundred arrived:** they emailed the Startup Vitamins list — an audience already made
  of entrepreneurs, which is to say exactly the people about to open a store. Then the Shopify
  integration, then press. Launch ad budget was around five thousand dollars.
- **Where it got to:** $289M revenue in 2021; Latvia's first unicorn at a $1B valuation that year.
- **Copy this:** the existing audience *was* the launch channel. This is the same move the journal on
  this site is making — build the list before the product needs it.

### PageFly — the same product, on a bigger platform

- **Founded** 2017 in Hanoi, by the agency BraveBits (est. 2007). Bootstrapped, no outside funding.
- **The origin:** the cleanest "existing asset, new platform" case here. BraveBits already sold JSN
  PageBuilder 2, a top-three page builder **for Joomla**. It was successful and rotting — a mix of
  jQuery, Underscore, Backbone and React where, in the CTO's description, every new feature had to be
  designed and researched from scratch. They rebuilt the core, shipped it as JSN Page Builder 3, and
  shipped the same core as **PageFly v1 for Shopify**.
- **The merchant's sentence:** "I can't build a decent landing page without paying a developer or
  hacking my theme's Liquid."
- **Where it got to:** 230,000+ merchants and 3,000,000+ pages (company site, Aug 2026, self-reported
  and internally inconsistent with their own older figures). **6.4%** of all Shopify stores.
- **Copy this:** platform arbitrage beats invention. A working product on a small platform, rebuilt for
  a bigger one, outran the original by an order of magnitude. Ask what you already have that is in the
  wrong market.

### Instafeed — the boring utility nobody writes a myth about

- **Launched** on the App Store 30 September 2016 by **Mintt Studio**, a small studio in Portugal with
  a narrow portfolio (Instafeed, TikTok Feed, Free Gift).
- **Honest gap:** no founder name, founding narrative, funding status or first-customer account exists
  in any accessible public source. Rather than reconstruct one, note what that absence itself tells you.
- **The merchant's sentence:** "My Instagram is my best-looking content and none of it is on my store."
- **Where it got to:** 4.9/5 from 2,136 reviews, Built for Shopify status, a free tier with no branding,
  and **8.0%** of all Shopify stores — the fifth most-installed app on the platform. No revenue data
  is public.
- **Copy this, and it may be the most encouraging entry here:** the best solo-builder niche can be a
  boring, permanently-recurring, single-job utility that nobody wants to write a founder story about.
  Ten years of compounding App Store reviews on one high-intent generic search term is the moat. There
  is no narrative, no funding, no pivot — just one job done well for a decade.

### Recharge — six products before the one that stuck

- **Founded** 2014, Santa Monica, by Oisin O'Connor and Mike Flynn (O'Connor's own telling mentions
  starting it "with two friends"; a third co-founder is never named in press materials). Bootstrapped
  for five years, then $50M Series A (2020) and $277M Series B at a **$2.1B valuation** (May 2021).
- **The origin:** they ran an ecommerce agency and their own Shopify store, shipping app after app to
  fund themselves. O'Connor's framing is that the agency let them see the problems everyone in the
  ecosystem was having around recurring billing for physical products. Reporting says they built **six
  different products** before the subscription one stuck.
- **The merchant's sentence:** "Shopify takes a one-off payment fine, but I can't bill the same
  customer every 30 days for my coffee without hacking it together."
- **Where it got to:** 20,000+ brands, 100M+ subscribers, **$100M+ ARR**, $30B+ processed. Acquired
  **Skio** in April 2026.
- **Copy this:** run an agency or a store as both the funding and the research channel. The thing you
  keep charging clients to hand-fix is the product. Six shipped products before the winner is the
  normal number, not a failure.
- **Read this one twice.** This is the incumbent the Zero-Churn Switch offer is aimed at, and the
  percentage fee that merchants now complain about is the same revenue model that made it worth $2.1B.
  That is exactly the *structural* weakness from [12's question 4](12-PROBLEM-SELECTION.md#4-the-five-question-painkiller-test):
  they cannot remove it without breaking themselves.

### Smile.io — right product, wrong platform

- **Founded** as **Sweet Tooth** in Waterloo, Ontario, by four co-founders in their final year of
  university in 2011, out of an agency started in 2009. One seed round of CDN$2.25M in 2012.
- **The origin:** they built custom ecommerce sites, then Magento extensions, and noticed small
  merchants had no access to the loyalty programmes big retailers ran. The first version ran **over
  email with no real interface** and failed on demand after eight months. By late 2013 the agency was
  dying — they took a loan to keep paying employees and were, by their own account, a week or two from
  shutting down.
- **The merchant's sentence:** "The big chains have a points card that brings people back. I have
  nothing and I can't build one."
- **The pivot was the platform, not the idea.** They saw Shopify gaining on Magento and shipped their
  first Shopify app in 2014 — points, VIP tiers, referrals. That is when the growth started.
- **Where it got to:** 100,000+ loyalty programmes powered; ARR tracking toward US$10M around the 2021
  rebrand; still independent. *A third-party database lists $1.8M ARR, which contradicts the 2021
  figure and should not be trusted.*
- **Copy this:** before killing an idea that is not selling, try shipping the same idea where the buyers
  are already installing things. The product was right and the platform was wrong — eight months of
  failure looked identical to a bad idea.

### Rebuy — build what one paying client asked for

- **Founded** 2017, Minneapolis, by brothers John Erck and James Van Erck. Seed $4.4M (2021), Series A
  $17M (Feb 2023). Angels include Ben Jabbawy — the Privy founder from earlier in this file.
- **The origin:** both engineers, in the Shopify ecosystem since 2010, having run their own D2C brands
  and then a Shopify agency. The trigger was a single client — **Primal Kitchen** — asking them to
  intelligently cross-sell products inside email campaigns. The custom build lifted AOV, other clients
  wanted it, and they productised it.
- **The merchant's sentence:** "My best customer is the one I already have, and my store shows them
  the same generic 'you may also like' block as a stranger."
- **v1** was a repeat-order app — hence the name — that later grew into cart, post-purchase offers and
  search.
- **Where it got to:** ~7,000 stores and eight-figure ARR at the Series A; revenue grew 20x monthly
  through 2020 while the team went from 3 to 12. Company now claims 50,000+ brands. Also one of the
  fastest-growing apps in this repo's own trend data (**+28.3% YoY**).
- **Copy this:** v1 was revenue-validated before it was a product. Build the custom thing one paying
  client asked for, then sell it to the next twenty who ask for the same thing.

### Postscript — one sentence over lunch

- **Founded** October 2018, Scottsdale, by Adam Turner, Alex Beller and Colin Turner. Y Combinator W19.
  $106M+ raised, Series C $65M (June 2022).
- **The origin:** Adam and Alex met at StackCommerce running white-label stores for publishers, where
  they watched email click-through rates fall while mobile engagement rose. The concrete trigger was a
  friend at lunch in 2018 saying he ran a Shopify store and **wished he could just text his customers**.
- **The merchant's sentence:** "Nobody opens my emails any more, but everybody reads their texts — and
  I have no compliant way to text my buyers."
- **v1** was, in their own words, Mailchimp for texting: one-way broadcast SMS for a single Shopify
  store, for product drops and launch announcements.
- **How the first hundred arrived:** deliberately targeting indie sellers, won through obsessive live
  chat — founders answering at night, refining the product from those conversations, and turning that
  service into App Store reviews and word of mouth.
- **Where it got to:** 8,500 brands (Jan 2023) → 16,000+; 400% YoY growth through 2022; $500M+ in
  attributed merchant revenue. *A "$656M valuation" figure circulates in aggregator blogs — unverified.*
- **Copy this:** one line from one real merchant, heard while you happen to have the context to act on
  it, beats a market study. Then out-support everyone in a channel where reviews *are* the distribution.

### Triple Whale — the dashboard they built for their own brand

- **Founded** 2021 by Maxx Blank, AJ Orbach and Ivan Chernykh, all former DTC operators. $52.7M raised,
  including a $25M Series B (Feb 2023) with strategic participation from **Shopify** itself.
- **The origin:** Maxx and his wife had built the hair-accessory brand **Madison Braids** to eight
  figures, and his description of the worst part is hours of manual work typing data into spreadsheets
  to get insight. They built the dashboard internally for their own brands, other DTC founders asked
  for access, and it became a SaaS. iOS 14 had just broken everyone's tracking, which supplied urgency.
- **The merchant's sentence:** "Every morning I rebuild the same spreadsheet from Shopify, Facebook and
  Google, and I still don't know which ad made the sale."
- **How the first hundred arrived:** cold DMs to DTC operators on Twitter and recruiting early testers,
  then an inflection when Shaan Puri tweeted it to roughly 350,000 followers.
- **Where it got to:** 5,000+ brands and $15B+ GMV by 2023, 1,300% YoY growth in 2022; now claims
  60,000+ brands. **11.4%** of Plus stores.
- **Copy this:** the strongest wedge available to a solo builder is the tool you already run your own
  operation on, and the distribution is the operator community you are already in — not ads. Note the
  cold DMs came *first*; the big tweet only amplified something that already worked.

### Bold Commerce — four people with day jobs, in a basement

- **Founded** 2012, Winnipeg, by Jay Myers, Stefan Maynard and brothers Eric and Yvan Boisjoli.
  Bootstrapped for seven years.
- **The origin:** Myers had sold his family's archery products online since 2005 and concluded that
  selling on eBay meant renting customers. He went looking in the young Shopify App Store for a
  product-comparison tool, found nothing, brainstormed about ten app ideas with Maynard in a pub, and
  recruited the Boisjoli brothers to build. All four kept their day jobs, working roughly 5pm to 3am
  from a basement. **Product Upsell launched 18 April 2012.**
- **The best detail in this entire file:** their first customer left a negative review within five
  minutes, because they had forgotten to upload the instructions page. Myers' view is that the review
  was the best thing that ever happened to them — it woke them up.
- **The merchant's sentence:** "I want to offer a bigger or extra item the moment someone adds to cart,
  and there is no way to do it."
- **How the first hundred arrived:** the App Store itself, into a marketplace young enough that a
  boring, obviously-monetising feature had no competitor. They had more requests than they could
  handle. Eric quit his job first in October 2012, Maynard in November, Yvan in January 2013, Myers in
  March 2013 — in that order, as revenue allowed.
- **Where it got to:** 90,000+ brands across their apps; later cut from 36 products to 17 to focus.
- **Copy this, and this is the most copyable story here:** launch while employed, at night, and let the
  revenue decide when you quit. The edge was timing and merchant experience, not technology.

### Okendo — keep the insight, change the wrapper

- **Founded** 2018, Sydney, by Matt Goodman and Matt Garven. *Sources disagree on the year (2017, 2018,
  2019).* Bootstrapped and profitable until a $5.3M seed led by Index (July 2021), then a $26M Series A
  (July 2022).
- **The origin — and a correction worth recording.** It is often said Okendo started as an agency.
  **That is not supported by any source found.** What is documented: Goodman and Garven had already
  built **VidTitan**, a Shopify app for collecting video testimonials. They found ecommerce was where it
  got traction and evolved it into Okendo. It was a pivot from their own earlier product, not an agency
  spin-out.
- **The merchant's sentence:** "I'm collecting star ratings, but they're generic and they don't tell a
  shopper anything that makes them buy."
- **Where it got to:** 3,500+ DTC brands by mid-2021, 5,000+ by mid-2022, 18,000+ claimed today.
  **6.1%** of Plus stores.
- **Copy this:** the second product beat the first because it was the same insight aimed at where money
  was already moving. Keep the insight, change the wrapper.

### Yotpo — one widget, every marketplace at once

- **Founded** 2011, Tel Aviv, by Tomer Tagrin and Omri Cohen, who met at university. Venture-funded
  from the start, through to a $230M Series F at a **$1.4B valuation** (March 2021).
- **The origin:** the repeated story is that Tagrin was misled by inauthentic online reviews and built a
  platform for verified, social ones. *This appears only in secondary write-ups, never in a primary
  founder interview — treat it as the company legend rather than a sourced fact.*
- **The merchant's sentence:** "Nobody leaves reviews on my store, and the ones I have look fake."
- **How the first hundred arrived — the actual mechanism:** plugins, not sales. By October 2012 Yotpo
  was on 2,000 commerce sites and adding about 120 a week, integrated with Magento, BigCommerce,
  Shopify, Volusion and 3dCart. Breadth across every cart in existence was the engine.
- **Where it got to:** over $100M ARR and ~30,000 paying customers by the 2021 Series F.
- **Copy this:** a small widget distributed through every marketplace at once beats a better widget on
  one. Note this is the exact opposite of PageFly's lesson — both worked, which should tell you the
  distribution channel matters more than which theory of it you hold.

### Attentive — the wedge was the opt-in, not the messages

- **Founded** late 2016, New York, by Brian Long and Andrew Jones, both from TapCommerce, which sold to
  Twitter for a reported ~$100M.
- **The origin, and it was a pivot:** the first idea was software for businesses to communicate with
  frontline workers. About six months of lukewarm response led them to what buyers actually wanted —
  mobile messaging with *customers*. Long's public thesis was that people no longer want to download an
  app, and messaging is a cheap, effective way to manage the customer relationship.
- **v1 was not a feature-rich SMS platform.** It was a two-tap opt-in flow using mobile deep links that
  pre-filled the phone number. They solved list **growth** first and messaging second.
- **Where it got to:** $2.2B valuation (Sept 2020), $470M Series E at ~$7B (June 2021), $500M ARR
  announced for 2024. Also the company that **acquired Privy** in 2021.
- **Copy this — the wedge, not the path.** Own the step everyone else treats as boring setup. But be
  honest about the rest: they came out of stealth with 50+ customers already live and a $13M Series A,
  because a Twitter exit bought them both. **This is the least copyable story in this file.** Read it
  for the opt-in insight and ignore the trajectory.

## 3. When Shopify ships the thing you built

Every solo builder's first fear. The record is more interesting than the fear.

**What Shopify has shipped for free:** Shopify Email (globally April 2020, filling the hole Mailchimp
left when it pulled its integration in 2019 — 2,500 sends/month free, then $1 per 1,000). Shopify Ping
and Chat relaunched as **Shopify Inbox**, free on every plan. **Shopify Forms** free on 8 Nov 2022 —
popups and email capture wired straight into Shopify Email and segmentation. Shopify Bundles free in
July 2023. Translate & Adapt alongside Markets. In July 2026, native WhatsApp campaigns, UPS return
labels and unlimited multi-currency payouts.

**And yet: no named third-party app can be verified to have shut down because Shopify shipped a native
feature.** Anyone telling that story cleanly is asserting more than the public record supports. What is
documented is softer and more useful:

- A developer's 2023 post on leaving the Shopify apps business notes Shopify runs around 38 of its own
  apps, with Shopify Reviews and Collabs dominating their categories. But his actual existential event
  was **enforcement**, not competition: one of his apps was banned and he was locked out of another.
  His line about always being one support agent or automated bot away from losing your business is the
  real risk in this ecosystem.
- A 2024 post titled "The Shopify App Store Is Broken" is widely cited as first-party criticism. Read
  accurately, its complaint is **free apps dominating review-weighted rankings** — not Shopify
  competing.
- One operator's July 2026 estimate is that native features launch at roughly 60% of the depth of the
  app they displace, and the missing 40% is almost always segmentation, reporting and edge cases.
  *One person's estimate, not measured data.*

**Two counter-examples that matter more than the scare stories:**

1. **Shopify's 2020 subscription APIs did not kill subscription apps.** Recharge, Bold and Ordergroove
   migrated onto native checkout, and a whole wave of new entrants — Skio, Smartrr, Upscribe — was
   *born* on those same APIs. Platform features create categories at least as often as they end them.
2. **Shopify retired its own free Product Reviews app on 6 May 2024**, reportedly with 500k+ installs,
   explicitly handing the category to Judge.me, Okendo, Loox and Stamped. Shopify exits categories too.
   It also killed its own Kit app in August 2021.

**What to take from this:** first-party risk is real but mispriced. The genuine existential risk in the
record is account and App Store enforcement, not feature competition. Build where the native version's
missing 40% — segmentation, reporting, edge cases, migration — is the whole reason merchants pay. And
never let your only distribution channel be the one that can ban you. That last sentence is the
strongest argument this repo has for the journal, the email list and direct outreach.

## 4. The logic underneath: four ways a problem gets found

Strip the fourteen stories of their outcomes and only four starting shapes remain. None of them is
"I brainstormed app ideas." Find yourself in one of these.

### A. The agency pattern — the problem you keep charging clients to hand-fix

**Recharge · Rebuy · Smile.io · PageFly**

You do client work. The same manual fix keeps appearing on different invoices. That repetition *is*
the market research, and it is already paid for. Rebuy's v1 was one custom cross-sell build for Primal
Kitchen; Recharge shipped six products off the back of agency pattern-recognition before subscriptions
stuck.

**The tell:** you have already been paid to solve it at least twice.
**The trap:** agency revenue is comfortable enough to postpone productising forever.

### B. The operator itch — the chore you personally do every morning

**Triple Whale · Printful · Oberlo**

You run a store. Something eats an hour a day. You build the internal tool, and other operators ask for
access. Triple Whale was a dashboard for Madison Braids; Oberlo was a CSV job its founders did every
morning; Printful was a garage printer for their own poster business.

**The tell:** you built it for yourself before anyone asked.
**The trap:** your workflow may be unusual. Ten strangers with the same chore is the proof, not your own
relief.

### C. The overheard sentence — someone else's complaint, in their words

**Postscript · Klaviyo · Loop Returns · Bold Commerce**

You are not the sufferer; you are close enough to hear it and equipped to act. Postscript came from a
friend at lunch wishing he could just text his customers. Klaviyo came from helping a friend's store.
Loop came from two named brands drowning in manual returns. Bold came from Myers searching the App
Store for a tool that did not exist.

**The tell:** you can quote the sentence, and it is not yours.
**The trap:** one sentence is an anecdote. [12's workshop](12-PROBLEM-SELECTION.md) exists to turn it
into a count.

### D. The usage pivot — the users tell you what you built

**Gorgias · Privy · Okendo · Attentive**

You shipped something general. You looked at who actually used it and followed them. Gorgias found 70%
of its templated-reply users were ecommerce support agents. Privy found people valued the email capture,
not the promotions. Okendo found video testimonials worked in ecommerce. Attentive found frontline-worker
messaging was the wrong buyer.

**The tell:** the data disagrees with your pitch, and you believe the data.
**The trap:** it needs users first, so it is the only one of the four you cannot start from today.

> **The one thing all four share.** In every single story the problem was **already costing someone
> money before a line of product code existed** — in agency fees, in an hour a day, in a $199 app, in
> lost returns revenue. Not one of these companies created a new want.

## 5. And four ways the first hundred customers arrived

Origin gets you a product. This gets you a business, and it is where solo builders actually die.

| Pattern | Who did it | What it actually was |
|---|---|---|
| **The weekly habit** | **Privy** | Every Friday morning for 2+ years: Facebook groups, Shopify/Squarespace/Mailchimp forums, Quora searches on the same terms, any podcast at all. ~50 installs month one, doubling monthly |
| **The list you already own** | **Printful** | Emailed the Startup Vitamins list — an audience of founders, i.e. future store owners. ~$5,000 of launch ads |
| **Clients first** | **Rebuy · Recharge · Smile** | Sell it to the client who asked, then the next twenty who ask the same thing |
| **Out-support everyone** | **Postscript · Bold** | Founders answering live chat at night, turning service into App Store reviews. Bold's first review was negative within five minutes, and they treated it as the wake-up |
| **Marketplace breadth** | **Yotpo** | One widget on Magento, BigCommerce, Shopify, Volusion, 3dCart at once: 2,000 sites, +120/week by 2012 |
| **Search + free tier** | **Judge.me · Instafeed** | One high-intent generic query, a real free plan, ten years of compounding reviews |
| **Cold DMs, then amplification** | **Triple Whale** | Cold DMs to DTC operators on Twitter *first*; the 350k-follower tweet only amplified something already working |

**Read that table against your own week.** Every one of these is unglamorous, repetitive and
un-automatable, and every single one is a *recurring calendar slot* rather than a launch. None of them
is an ad budget. Privy's version — one morning a week, for two years — is the cheapest thing on this
page and the one most likely to be skipped.

## 7. Five things to take away

1. **Nobody started with a category.** They started with one merchant, one invoice, or one chore.
2. **The problem was already being paid for.** A $199 app, an agent's salary, an hour every morning.
   Undercutting the cost of an existing workaround beats inventing a new want, every time.
3. **v1 was embarrassingly small.** An opt-in flow. A rewards system with no interface. Products
   copy-pasted by hand. A first review that was negative within five minutes.
4. **Distribution was a habit, not an event.** Friday mornings for two years. Live chat at night. Cold
   DMs before the viral tweet.
5. **Boring wins more often than brilliant.** The most-installed app on Shopify is the cheap version of
   a proven category. The fifth is a utility with no founder story at all.

**For this repo specifically:** the offer, the page and the analysis are done, and the archetype is
clear — a structural weakness in an incumbent, which is the strongest wedge in this entire file. What
is missing is §5. The prospect list exists. The Friday morning does not yet.
